| Early dopamine elevation modifies the behavioral consequences of maternal separation in female and male juvenile mice | Reis, A. L. d. A. L. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.07.757433v1.full.pdf) | Alterations in dopaminergic signaling and exposure to early-life stress are both implicated in the developmental origins of psychiatric disorders, yet little is known about how these factors interact during sensitive periods of brain maturation. We investigated whether increased dopamine availability during early postnatal development modifies the behavioral consequences of maternal separation in female and male C57BL/6 mice. From postnatal day (PD) 1 to PD14, mice received L-Dopa plus benserazide, maternal separation (3 h/day), their combination, or the corresponding control conditions. We assessed mice behavior between PD28 and PD32 using novel object recognition, open-field, elevated plus-maze, marble-burying, and forced-swim tests, followed by hippocampal volumetric analysis. Early L-Dopa exposure produced sex- and behavior-specific effects. In females, L-Dopa increased rearing without altering horizontal locomotion and modified open-arm exploration and stress-coping behavior, whereas in males it increased open-arm exploration and reduced climbing frequency. Maternal separation produced distinct effects in females and males and reduced repetitive digging/burying behavior in both sexes. Importantly, maternal separation frequently modified rather than potentiated the effects of L-Dopa. In both sexes, combined maternal separation and L-Dopa opposed several L-Dopa-associated alterations in open-field, elevated plus-maze, and forced-swim behaviors. Recognition memory was largely preserved across experimental conditions. L-Dopa also reduced hippocampal volume selectively in females, whereas hippocampal volume was higher in females exposed to maternal separation plus L-Dopa than in those receiving L-Dopa alone. Multivariate analyses revealed overlapping rather than treatment-specific behavioral clusters, indicating substantial individual heterogeneity and sex-dependent shifts in multidimensional behavioral profiles. Together, these findings demonstrate that transient alterations in dopamine availability and maternal separation interact non-additively during early postnatal development. Their consequences depend on sex, behavioral domain, and developmental timing, supporting a model in which neurochemical and environmental perturbations dynamically shape behavioral trajectories rather than simply producing cumulative developmental damage. |
| Spontaneous Hemodynamic Oscillations during Sleep Coordinate Directional CSF Flow in the Subarachnoid Space and Ventricles | Chen, D. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.07.757422v1.full.pdf) | During sleep, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow is enhanced and thought to facilitate clearance of metabolic waste from the brain. Human imaging has shown that sleep is associated with large waves of bulk CSF flow in the fourth ventricle; however, the CSF flow dynamics around brain tissue are unknown. Here, we investigated how CSF flow is organized across the subarachnoid space, a key site both for fluid exchange across cortical surface and for transport out of the cranium. We used an MRI technology at 7 Tesla that simultaneously measures cortical hemodynamics and slow subarachnoid CSF flow and applied this during sleep. We found that spontaneous infraslow hemodynamic oscillations were coupled to spatially structured, directional CSF flow throughout both ventricular and subarachnoid compartments. Hemodynamic oscillations and fourth-ventricle bulk flow were both associated with brain-wide CSF flow at different phases, indicating that they reflect a single coordinated CSF movement cycle. Compared to cardiac pulsations, a canonical driver of CSF movement, hemodynamic-driven CSF flow showed a distinct directional pattern and was smaller in instantaneous amplitude but produced substantially greater cumulative displacement and thus greater potential for transport within an oscillation period. Together, these findings show that hemodynamic oscillations during sleep are coupled to temporally coordinated and directional CSF flow throughout the ventricles and subarachnoid space, suggesting that sleep-dependent neuronal activity drives fluid movement across the human brain in a distinct spatial pattern. |
| Co-option of CENP-A for activity-induced neuronal plasticity | Stankovic, A. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.16.738894v1.full.pdf) | Neuronal activation drives activity-dependent gene expression that underlies experience-associated synaptic modifications, learning and memory. Neuronal activation increases a non-centromeric nuclear pool of CENP-A while leaving centromeric CENP-A levels unchanged. Despite extensive knowledge of dystrophin function, the cellular mechanisms responsible for progressive muscle degeneration remain poorly understood. Methods: We investigated sarcolemma integrity, membrane repair capacity, and annexin protein expression in DMD patient muscle biopsies and human skeletal muscle cell lines using immunohistochemistry, both shear stress-based and laser irradiation injury assays, Western blotting, and live-cell imaging of GFP-tagged annexins. To assess whether extracellular annexin A2 (ANXA2) is associated with disease severity, we further analyzed muscle biopsies from patients with Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) and compared the mdx and D2-mdx mouse models. Results: We identified defective membrane repair in DMD skeletal muscle cells, independent of increased membrane fragility, by evaluating resealing capacity in control and DMD patient-derived cell lines using both a shear stress assay (N = 12, p < 0.0001) and a laser irradiation assay (N = 3, p < 0.0001). Analyses performed on human DMD muscle biopsies (N = 10) further confirmed this defect, demonstrating massive intracellular IgG uptake (p < 0.0001) together with altered annexin expression profiles. While mechanical stress induces the upregulation of annexin A5 (ANXA5, p < 0.01) and A6 (ANXA6, p < 0.05) in healthy skeletal muscle cells - suggesting an adaptive response to membrane damage - we observed dysregulated expression patterns of these proteins in DMD cells. Notably, ANXA1 (p < 0.05) and ANXA2 (p < 0.01) were not only significantly overexpressed but also aberrantly localized to the extracellular space, a putative consequence of defective membrane repair. Importantly, extracellular ANXA2 was progressively increased with disease severity, showing intermediate levels in BMD (N = 3, p < 0.05) compared with DMD biopsies and greater accumulation in D2-mdx than in mdx mice (N = 3, p < 0.05), identifying extracellular ANXA2 as a marker closely associated with dystrophinopathy severity. Conclusions: Our findings identify defective membrane repair as a central pathogenic mechanism in human DMD and indicate that the extracellular accumulation of ANXA2 results from impaired membrane repair and is associated with disease severity. Together, these findings suggest that extracellular annexins may contribute to disease progression and represent potential therapeutic targets, provided that their extracellular functions can be selectively inhibited without compromising their essential intracellular role in membrane repair. |

We developed Bayesian hierarchical spatial models to predict county-level relative abundance of Amblyomma americanum, Dermacentor variabilis, and Ixodes scapularis using surveillance data from 2019-2022. Relative abundance was defined as the species-specific share of the collected focal ticks and modeled using beta-binomial models that incorporated climate, land cover, forest fragmentation, deer habitat suitability, and BYM2 spatial effects in INLA. Statewide predictions extended inference to counties lacking species-specific observations and identified where uncertainty remained greatest, providing a basis for prioritizing additional surveillance across Illinois. |
| Rethink the Sink: Urban cores act as leaky sinks to maintain regional gene flow in coyotes | Vance, S. E. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.24.753963v1.full.pdf) | Urbanization is an accelerating evolutionary force, yet the patterns and drivers of urban gene flow remain largely obscured. This leaves a critical gap in testing the interplay between demographic and genetic models of urban connectivity. Specifically: the urban fragmentation model, where structural barriers restrict gene flow and drive genetic drift; the urban facilitation model, where permeable matrices enhance functional connectivity; and the urban sink hypothesis, a demographic model where high mortality creates unsustainable population sinks. We used high-resolution RAD-capture data to conduct landscape genomics and gene flow analyses to resolve these models and investigate contemporary evolution in urban coyotes (Canis latrans) across the greater Los Angeles, CA, USA metropolitan area. By incorporating fine-scale environmental, social-ecological, and historical variables of the built environment, we rigorously assessed how urban heterogeneity shapes intra-city evolutionary dynamics. We found weak population structure, heavily influenced by family groups. There was extensive landscape permeability and gene flow across the study area including across four major highways. We also identified heavily asymmetric gene flow from exurban source populations into the urban core, supporting the urban sink paradigm. However, rather than acting as a genetic dead-end, the urban matrix showed striking functional heterogeneity: one urban core functioned as a 'genetic sponge,' absorbing unique transient alleles from multiple surrounding sources, and both urban cores operated as permeable 'leaky sinks,' exporting critical gene flow to other populations. Consequently, urban demographic sinks can simultaneously act as vital genetic bridges. Our findings reject a pure urban fragmentation model while demonstrating that demographic sink dynamics and genetic facilitation are not mutually exclusive. This demonstrates that high-turnover urban sinks can buffer regional biodiversity against genetic erosion, highlighting the need to prioritize matrix permeability and inter-patch connectivity in urban conservation strategies. |
| Exposure to paired blue light and nutrient alters aversive responses in Physarum polycephalum | Wang, Q. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.09.750388v1.full.pdf) | Whilst forms of non-associative learning such as habituation are widely reported in single-celled organisms, whether associative learning is possible in systems without a nervous system is contested. In this study, we implemented a conditioning paradigm in the acellular slime mould Physarum polycephalum through repeated pairings of nutrients with an aversive blue light stimulus. We first established that blue light is aversive under our conditioning protocol, slowing migration and producing broader, less directed growth, confirming its well documented effect on behaviour. Subjects were then trained in up to four training sessions by placing an oat stimulus inside a region with blue light before testing with the light presented alone. Trained subjects were more likely to reach the blue light region compared to untrained controls, with shorter latency, suggesting that Physarum weakens its avoidance of blue light after the region has repeatedly coincided with a nutrient. However, engagement did not strengthen with additional pairings, a pattern that admits multiple competing explanations in which associative and non-associative processes plausibly contribute to more transient associative-like effects. We conclude that a history of co-presentation is sufficient to relax an aversive response in Physarum but cannot, within the present study, attribute that change to associative learning. |
| Analysis of ribosomes from the Wild-type and FMR1 knockout human embryonic stem cells | Kharbanda, T. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.11.18.624098v1.full.pdf) | Fragile X Messenger Ribonucleoprotein 1 (FMRP) is a multifunctional, multidomain RNA-binding protein whose loss causes Fragile X syndrome. It is also known to associate with ribosomes and modulate translation. In human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), knockout (KO) of the FMR1 gene results in significantly increased protein translation rates and alterations in the 2'-O-methylation patterns of rRNA. To understand the structural underpinnings of the process, we performed structural analysis of ribosomes isolated from both the wild-type (WT) and the FMR1 KO hESCs, which revealed populations of ribosomes with distinct protein factors and tRNA in the FMR1 KO ribosome preparations. The bound protein factors are hallmarks of dormant ribosomes, which are currently not observed in the WT hESCs, and are characterized by the binding of SERPINE-mRNA binding protein 1 in the mRNA tunnel and eukaryotic elongation factor 2 near the A-site. The observation of elevated protein translation in FMR1 KO cells, alongside a subpopulation of dormant ribosomes, further substantiates the role of FMRP in translational regulation. Additionally, we analysed the 2'-O-methylation patterns in the 28S rRNA, in both the WT and the FMR1 KO hESCs, identifying a few potential differentially methylated sites. Thus, these observations provide insights into the mechanisms of ribosome dormancy in the absence of FMRP and in understanding the role of rRNA methylation in translational regulation. |
| Pulsations of engineered microvessels enhance solute clearance via heterogeneous strain-dependent poroelasticity | Alcaide Martin, D. | 2026-10-14 | [PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.08.698435v1.full.pdf) | Tissue homeostasis relies on the exchange of interstitial fluid between blood and tissues, as well as lymphatic drainage. In regions lacking conventional lymphatic vasculature, such as the central nervous system, the mechanisms governing interstitial fluid clearance remain debated, with vascular pulsatility proposed as a potential driver of interstitial fluid transport. Using a microvessel-on-chip platform that enables pulsatile pressure actuation and real-time visualization of deformation and interstitial solute dynamics in a tissue-mimetic collagen hydrogel, we investigated how pulsatile actuation regulates solute transport. Sinusoidal pressure stimulation produced a flattened but nearly symmetric deformation response in acellular lumens, whereas HUVEC-vascularized lumens exhibited pronounced asymmetry between expansion and retraction, accompanied by strain accumulation in the surrounding hydrogel. In vascularized systems, but not acellular controls, pulsatile actuation enhanced solute clearance and increased the cycle-averaged solute front velocity compared with constant-pressure actuation. To investigate the underlying mechanism, we developed a one-dimensional large-deformation poroelastic model of the vascularized bilayer. The model reproduced net directional fluid transport and showed that bilayer poromechanical heterogeneity combined with strain-dependent nonlinear permeability generates an influx-efflux asymmetry and net fluid flow. Together, these findings identify how pulsatile pressure within microvessels can generate directional interstitial fluid transport and enhance solute clearance, potentially contributing to tissue homeostasis. |
